## Physical Connections
Before any network communication can take place, a physical connection to the local network must be established. This can be wired or wireless. For wireless connections, devices associate with a wireless access point (AP) or wireless integrated router.

#### Wireless Access Points (AP)
Components:
1. Wireless antennas (often integrated internally)
2. Ethernet switchports
3. An internal router port

![[Pasted image 20260127070956.png]]

#### Network Interface Cards (NICs)
NICs connect an end device to the network medium. Ethernet NICs are used for wired links, while WLAN (Wireless Local Area Network) NICs are used for wireless connectivity.
- End-user computers often contain both wired Ethernet and WLAN NICs.
- Network printers may only possess an Ethernet port.
- Smartphones and tablets typically contain only WLAN/cellular radios.

# Physical Layer
The Physical Layer of the OSI Model (or Network Access layer of [[3.1 TCP-IP]]) provides the electrical, optical, and mechanical means to transport the raw bits that constitute a Data Link frame across network media. It accepts a complete frame from the Data Link layer and encodes it as a continuous series of physical signals.

![[Pasted image 20260127072048.png]]

### Physical Layer Standards Organizations
- **ISO** (International Organization for Standardization)
- **TIA/EIA** (Telecommunications Industry Association / Electronic Industries Alliance)
- **ITU** (International Telecommunication Union)
- **ANSI** (American National Standards Institute)
- **IEEE** (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers)
- National regulatory bodies (FCC in the USA, ETSI in Europe)
- Regional cabling standards: CSA (Canada), CENELEC (Europe), JSA/JIS (Japan).

Physical layer standards govern three functional areas:
1. **Physical Components**: Connectors, jacks, pinouts, transceivers, cable shielding, and hardware specifications.
2. **Encoding**: Converting a stream of raw data bits into a standardized transmission code pattern recognizable by transmitter and receiver.
   - For example, **Manchester encoding** (used in legacy 10 Mbps 10BASE-T) represents a bit 0 with a high-to-low voltage transition, and a bit 1 with a low-to-high transition in the middle of the bit interval.
   - Higher data rates employ more advanced line encoding schemes: 100BASE-TX uses 4B/5B encoding, while 1000BASE-T employs 8B/10B or PAM-5.

### Bandwidth
Bandwidth measures the maximum capacity of a physical medium to carry data over time. Digital bandwidth measures how many bits can flow from one point to another in a given time interval.
In both 10 Mbps and 100 Mbps Ethernet, electrical signals travel across copper at roughly the speed of electricity; the difference lies in the density and frequency of bits transmitted per second.

| Unit of Bandwidth | Abbreviation | Equivalence in bps |
|-------------------|--------------|---------------------|
| Bits per second | bps | 1 bps (fundamental unit) |
| Kilobits per second | kbps | $1,000\text{ bps} = 10^3\text{ bps}$ |
| Megabits per second | Mbps | $1,000,000\text{ bps} = 10^6\text{ bps}$ |
| Gigabits per second | Gbps | $1,000,000,000\text{ bps} = 10^9\text{ bps}$ |
| Terabits per second | Tbps | $1,000,000,000,000\text{ bps} = 10^{12}\text{ bps}$ |

#### Bandwidth Terminology
- **Latency**: The elapsed time (including queueing and serialization delays) for data to traverse from source to destination. Across an internetwork, end-to-end throughput is bottlenecked by the slowest link along the transmission path.
- **Throughput**: The actual volume of bits successfully transferred across a medium within a given time period. In real-world networks, throughput is lower than theoretical bandwidth due to network congestion, protocol overhead, and latency.
- **Goodput**: The measure of usable application data transferred per unit time:
  $$\text{Goodput} = \text{Throughput} - \text{Protocol Overhead (Headers, Handshakes, ACKs, Retransmissions)}$$
  $$\text{Goodput} < \text{Throughput} \le \text{Bandwidth}$$

## Copper Cabling
Inexpensive, easy to install, and low electrical resistance, but bounded by distance limits and signal degradation (attenuation).
Susceptible to two major forms of noise:
- **EMI (Electromagnetic Interference) and RFI (Radio Frequency Interference)**: Distorts signals; generated by motors, fluorescent ballasts, power lines, and radio transmitters.
- **Crosstalk**: Unwanted coupling of magnetic/electrical fields from one wire pair into an adjacent wire pair.

Mitigation:
- Shielding (foil/braid) grounded properly.
- Twisting wire pairs together: Current flowing in opposite directions in each wire of a circuit produces complementary opposing magnetic fields that cancel each other out (cancellation effect).

### Types of Copper Cables
1. **[[resources/UTP cable|UTP (Unshielded Twisted-Pair)]]**: Most common LAN media; terminated with RJ-45 modular connectors. 4 color-coded twisted pairs enclosed in a flexible jacket.
   ![[Pasted image 20260127082910.png]]
2. **STP (Shielded Twisted-Pair)**: Better EMI/RFI immunity and crosstalk reduction than UTP, but significantly more expensive, thicker, and harder to terminate. Requires proper electrical grounding.
   ![[Pasted image 20260127083401.png]]
3. **Coaxial Cable**: Two concentric conductors sharing a common physical axis (center copper core, dielectric insulator, woven copper braid shield, outer jacket). Terminated using BNC, F-type, or N-type connectors.
   - Still used for connecting wireless antennas and in DOCSIS cable internet services.

## UTP Cabling Standards
Defined by TIA/EIA-568:
- Category 5: 100 Mbps (100BASE-TX)
- Category 5e: 1,000 Mbps / 1 Gbps (1000BASE-T) — minimum acceptable standard today
- Category 6: 1 Gbps up to 100 m, 10 Gbps up to 55 m
- Category 6a: 10 Gbps up to full 100 m
- Category 7 / 8: High-frequency data center runs up to 40 Gbps

### Straight-Through vs Crossover Pinouts
- **Straight-Through**: Connects unlike devices (PC to Switch, Switch to Router).
- **Crossover**: Connects like devices (Switch to Switch, PC to PC, Router to Router).
- **Rollover (Console)**: Proprietary Cisco pinout used to connect a workstation COM/USB port to a console management port.
- Modern devices use **[[resources/auto MDI-X|auto MDI-X]]** to detect and automatically cross pin connections internally.

## Fiber-Optic Cabling
Employs modular [[resources/SFP|SFP]] transceivers on switch and router uplinks. Optical cables utilize two strands (one transmit, one receive) or single-strand BiDi.
![[Pasted image 20260104141527.png]]
![[Pasted image 20260104141606.png]]

##### Multimode Fiber (MMF)
![[Pasted image 20260104141719.png]]
- Larger core diameter (50–62.5 microns).
- Allows multiple angles (modes) of light rays from low-cost LED or VCSEL transmitters.
- Ideal for campus and intra-building runs up to 550 m.

##### Singlemode Fiber (SMF)
![[Pasted image 20260104141910.png]]
- Extremely small glass core (9 microns).
- Single straight ray of light driven by specialized infrared laser diodes.
- Traverses very long spans (up to 10–40 km or transoceanic submarine lines).
![[Pasted image 20260104142158.png]]

## Comparison: UTP vs Fiber Optic

| Attribute | UTP Copper | Fiber Optic |
|-----------|------------|-------------|
| **Cost** | Low cost media and terminations | Higher material and transceiver cost |
| **Distance** | 100 meters maximum channel limit | Kilometers to tens of kilometers |
| **EMI / RFI Immunity** | Vulnerable to external interference | Completely immune to electromagnetic noise |
| **Interface Hardware** | Inexpensive RJ-45 jacks | Modular [[resources/SFP|SFP / SFP+]] transceivers |
| **Security** | Emits slight electromagnetic radiation | Completely tap-proof against standard interception |

## Wireless Media
Transports electromagnetic radio frequency (RF) signals:
- **Coverage**: Obstacles, wall materials, and terrain attenuate RF energy.
- **Interference**: Competing RF sources (microwaves, cordless phones, neighboring Wi-Fi channels).
- **Security**: Open transmission medium requires strong WPA2/WPA3 enterprise encryption.
- **Shared Medium**: Wi-Fi operates in half-duplex using [[resources/CSMA-CA|CSMA/CA]]; client contention divides effective per-station bandwidth.

### Wireless Standards
- **Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11)**: Wireless LAN technology utilizing CSMA/CA.
- **Bluetooth (IEEE 802.15)**: WPAN device pairing up to 10–100 m.
- **Zigbee (IEEE 802.15.4)**: Low-power, low-bandwidth mesh protocol for IoT smart home sensors.
- **WiMAX (IEEE 802.16)**: Wireless broadband access.

- `show ip interface brief`: Verifies interface assignments and link statuses.
- Serial cable clocking: **DCE** cables provide the clock rate signal to synchronizing **DTE** interfaces.
- **Point of Presence (POP)**: The access point where an ISP provides customer demarcation and upstream connectivity.
- **Media Access Control (MAC) determinants**:
  1. How nodes share the physical media (contention vs scheduled).
  2. How the topology appears logically to the Data Link Layer (point-to-point vs shared multi-access).
- **Hub-and-spoke WAN topology**: Multiple remote spoke branches connect directly to a centralized corporate hub rather than directly to each other.
